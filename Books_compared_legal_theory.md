# Two "Tax Honesty" Books Compared — Legal-Theory Analysis

_A structured comparison of the income-tax legal arguments in:_

- **Book 1 — Dave Champion, _Income Tax: Shattering the Myths_ (© 2010)** — a 13‑chapter treatise.
- **Book 2 — Thomas Freed, _The American Tax Bible_ (2nd ed.)** — a scripture‑framed compilation (GENESIS, THE BOOK OF CRYER, THE BOOK OF JOHN, THE BOOK OF TOMMY, EXODUS, REVELATION, THE BOOK OF THE FREED, SALVATION), built partly from other people's court briefs (attorney **Tommy Cryer**'s trial *Memorandum at Law*, and the **John Kottmair / Save‑A‑Patriot Fellowship** line of research).

_Method: the full text of both books was read and their legal-theory arguments extracted with citations, then compared. This document reports **what the books argue**, faithfully and without endorsement. **Neither book's core thesis has ever prevailed in a federal court**; the U.S. courts, the IRS ("The Truth About Frivolous Tax Arguments"), and the Congressional research literature uniformly reject these positions, and several proponents named in the books (Irwin Schiff, Pete Hendrickson, and others) were convicted or enjoined. That legal reality is the essential backdrop against which the two books' internal agreements and disagreements are laid out below._

---

## Executive summary

Both books reach the **same bottom-line conclusion** — that the federal income tax, correctly read, does not reach the ordinary American earning a living in one of the fifty states — and they get there through a **largely overlapping toolkit**: the income tax is "really" an indirect excise; the 16th Amendment "conferred no new power"; the only person actually made *liable* in Subtitle A is the withholding agent collecting from foreigners (§1461); the statutory definitions of "income," "wages," "employee," "United States," and "includes" are all narrower than people assume; and enforcement (levy, summons, substitute returns) is procedurally defective or reserved to alcohol/tobacco/firearms matters. They cite a common spine of Supreme Court cases — *Brushaber, Stanton, Pollock, Flint, Stratton's Independence, Eisner v. Macomber, Merchants' Loan, Hylton, Butchers' Union, Hale v. Henkel* — and the same statutory-construction canons (*Gould, Billings, Goldenberg, Calamaro, Meese v. Keene*).

But their **organizing logic is different**, and in a few places they **genuinely disagree**:

- **Champion's spine is jurisdictional-by-scope**: the income excise is *valid and fully operative*, but it is an **international tax** on foreign persons' U.S.-source income (plus U.S. citizens abroad with foreign income). Most Americans are simply outside the taxed class — "nontaxpayers" — who are tricked into *volunteering* through forms and TINs. His hallmark is a careful, treatise-like method that **explicitly rejects** rival tax-protester theories (the 861 position, "expatriation," UCC, zero returns, "Cracking the Code").
- **Freed's spine is jurisdictional-by-defect**: the 16th Amendment contains **no "enabling enforcement clause,"** so Congress can write no law, and courts have **no subject-matter jurisdiction**, to enforce it as a *direct* tax — a structural argument Champion never makes. Freed then piles on additional, more radical claims Champion avoids (the IRS "is not an agency," tax payments go to the Federal Reserve not the Treasury, the graduated bracket system is the "2nd plank of the Communist Manifesto"), and treats a 2017 congressional "Constitutional Authority Statement" as a decisive confession.

The single **sharpest contrast** is temperament and rigor: Champion is a lumper who prunes the movement's weaker arguments; Freed is a splitter who assembles nearly all of them. Their **one clean substantive contradiction** concerns the **corporate income tax** (see Part V).

## Part I — The shared foundational thesis (narrative)

Strip away the packaging and both authors build on an identical five-step chassis:

1. **Two tax classes only.** The Constitution knows *direct* taxes (must be apportioned among the states — Art. I §2 cl.3, §9 cl.4) and *indirect* taxes ("duties, imposts and excises," must be geographically uniform — Art. I §8 cl.1). There is no third class. (Champion: "the two great tax classes"; Freed: impost/duty/excise are the only three indirect species.)

2. **The income tax is inherently an *excise*.** Both lean on *Brushaber v. Union Pacific R.R.*, 240 U.S. 1 (1916) and *Stanton v. Baltic Mining*, 240 U.S. 103 (1916) for the proposition that "taxation on income was in its nature an excise" and that the 16th Amendment "conferred no new power of taxation." Both quote *Stanton*'s "inherently belonged to the category of indirect taxation" line. Both use *Flint v. Stone Tracy*, *Stratton's Independence v. Howbert*, and *Pollock* to define an excise as a tax on a *privilege* or on *corporate activity measured by income*.

3. **"Income" is not everything you receive.** Both cite *Eisner v. Macomber*, *Doyle v. Mitchell Bros.*, *Towne v. Eisner*, and *Merchants' Loan & Trust v. Smietanka* for a narrow, corporate-profit-flavored meaning of "income" fixed to the 1909 Corporate Excise Tax Act — a realized *gain from a source*, not gross receipts, and not the fruits of ordinary labor.

4. **Labor is a right, not a taxable privilege.** Both cite *Butchers' Union v. Crescent City*, *Coppage v. Kansas*, and *Corn v. Fort* (and Freed adds 15 U.S.C. §17, "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce") to argue an excise cannot reach the exercise of the common right to work. Both cite *Hale v. Henkel* (individual vs. corporation) and *Economy Plumbing v. United States* (the "nontaxpayer" who is outside the revenue laws entirely).

5. **The liability actually falls on a collector, not the earner.** Both make the *withholding-agent* argument the load-bearing wall: the only provision in Subtitle A that says anyone is "liable" is **§1461** (the person required to deduct and withhold), and the "withholding agent" (**§7701(a)(16)**) is defined only around **§§1441–1443** — nonresident aliens and foreign corporations. Ordinary Americans, if they appear at all, appear only as *collectors*, never as *subjects*. Both invoke **Treasury Decision 2313** (issued just after *Brushaber*) as proof the 1913 tax was administered against nonresident aliens.

From that shared chassis both derive the same practical claim — **no statute makes an ordinary American working in the fifty states liable to file or pay** — and the same explanation for why everyone believes otherwise: a system of **self-classification through forms** (W-4, W-9, 1040, the TIN) that induces people to *declare themselves* into the taxed class under penalty of perjury.

## Part III — Where they agree, in depth

**1. The withholding-agent / §1461 keystone.** This is the strongest point of convergence and, in both books, the true center of gravity. Both authors independently conclude that the *entire* Subtitle A liability structure resolves to a single person — the withholding agent collecting tax from foreign persons — and that ordinary Americans are cast as involuntary tax *collectors* rather than tax *subjects*. Both cite the identical chain: §7701(a)(16) → §§1441/1442/1443 → §1461, reinforced by **T.D. 2313**. Freed's "shifting the burden / sales-tax collector" analogy and Champion's "three hemispheres" (U.S. person / foreign person / nontaxpayer) are two vocabularies for the same structural claim.

**2. The definitional method.** Both books run on statutory-definition literalism: a term Congress has defined may be read *only* as defined (*Meese v. Keene*, *Colautti v. Franklin*), and "includes/including" is read as a term of *limitation to a class* rather than enlargement (§7701(c); Champion adds 27 CFR 72.11's "do not exclude things … in the same general class"). Both apply this to "employee" (§3401(c) = government workers), "wages," "trade or business," and "United States." Both invoke strict construction against the government (*Gould v. Gould*: taxes are not extended "by implication").

**3. The Form 2555 / OMB argument.** Remarkably, both books independently make the same paperwork argument: that the Paperwork Reduction Act cross-reference table (26 CFR §602.101) historically tied the §1 / reg. 1.1-1 filing requirement to **Form 2555, "Foreign Earned Income" (OMB 1545-0067)** — a form "for U.S. citizens and resident aliens" reporting *foreign* income — rather than to Form 1040 (OMB 1545-0074), and that the IRS later "moved" or removed the entry to obscure this. This is a fingerprint of a shared movement source.

**4. Enforcement is procedurally void or misdirected.** Both attack the substitute-for-return (must be *signed* under §6020(b); Champion cites an IRS Chief Counsel memo, Freed a GAO letter and the §6020(b) form list), the levy (§6331 reaches only federal employees; a Notice of Levy compels no third party), and the summons (traceable to alcohol/tobacco/firearms authority; enforcement regs in 27 CFR). Champion's "internal revenue districts = customs ports" and Freed's "§7608(a) is Subtitle E only" are the same Subtitle-E/ATF insight from two angles.

**5. Shared case-law backbone.** See Part VI. The overlap in Supreme Court authority is extensive and, in places, verbatim (both quote the same *Stanton* and *Brushaber* passages).

## Part IV — Where they differ, in depth

**1. The 16th Amendment: limited-but-live vs. dead-on-arrival.** Champion treats the 16th Amendment as a *functioning* provision that does real, if narrow, work — it prevents courts from reclassifying an income *excise* as a direct tax merely because of the *source* of the income (his reading of *South Carolina v. Baker*'s "otherwise taxable"). Freed's signature move is that the Amendment carries **no "enabling enforcement clause"** (contrasting it with the "Congress shall have power to enforce" language in the 13th/14th/15th/18th/19th/23rd/24th/26th), so Congress can enact *no* enforcing legislation under it and courts have *no subject-matter jurisdiction* to enforce it as a direct tax. Champion never makes this argument and does not need it; his case is about the *scope* of a valid tax, not the *enforceability* of the Amendment.

**2. Treatise vs. brief-bank.** Champion writes a single authorial argument and is at pains to distinguish himself from the rest of the movement. Freed literally compiles other people's legal documents — Cryer's courtroom *Memorandum at Law* (used "verbatim"), Save-A-Patriot/Kottmair material, and a large set of **ready-to-file templates** (motions to quash, mandamus petitions, §7433 complaints, state-tax reply letters). This makes Freed broader and more litigation-oriented but also less internally consistent.

**3. Radical add-ons that Champion omits (and implicitly rejects).** Freed advances several claims Champion pointedly does not:
   - **"The IRS is not an agency"** (never created by statute; *Norton v. Shelby County*).
   - **Tax payments go to the Federal Reserve, not the Treasury** (§7809; *Lewis v. United States*).
   - **The graduated bracket system is unconstitutional "class legislation"** and the "2nd plank of the Communist Manifesto"; "geographical uniformity" is a judicial fraud.
   - **Void-for-vagueness** from the circuit split (Becraft/Critzer/Mallas) negating criminal willfulness.
   - **H.R. 1 (2017)** as a congressional confession.
   Champion's careful methodology — he devotes a whole chapter ("The Good, The Bad, And The Mistaken") to *debunking* movement theories — strongly implies he would treat some of these as precisely the overreach he warns against.

**4. The territorial/"tariff" theory.** Both use the §3121(e) territorial definition of "United States," but they weight it very differently. For Champion it is mainly a **FICA** argument plus the *Downes v. Bidwell* "here vs. there" doctrine; his income-tax case rests primarily on the *foreign-person/privilege* analysis. For Freed the territorial redefinition is a **central pillar** of the income tax itself: the 1913 Underwood-Simmons **Tariff** Act (§II-H) redefined "United States" to exclude the fifty states, making the income tax a *tariff laid only in the foreign jurisdiction*.

**5. The judiciary: corrupt vs. powerless.** Champion's remedy is political and populist — he attacks judicial *integrity* ("outcome-based law," steered case assignments, jury tampering) and urges **grand-jury refusal and trial-jury nullification** (*Brailsford*, *Moylan*), no-TIN banking, shunning, and even edges toward self-defense rhetoric and the 2nd Amendment. Freed attacks judicial *authority* — the courts lack jurisdiction — and his remedy is **paper**: file the right motion and force the court to disclose its constitutional basis or dismiss.

**6. The "voluntary" hooks differ.** Champion's deepest "why does signing matter" hook is the **13th Amendment**: the 13th bans *involuntary* servitude but not *voluntary* servitude, and signing the forms is the voluntary act. Freed's hook is **§3402(n)** and §3402(p): withholding is an "allowance" the employee grants and can revoke by certifying EXEMPT. Both make the citizen's *signature/election* the operative trap, but from different doctrinal pegs.

## Part V — Contradictions, and whether they are compatible

The user asked specifically whether apparent contradictions are actually *compatible approaches to the same underlying theory*. Taking each in turn:

**A. "16th Amendment functions" (Champion) vs. "16th Amendment is unenforceable" (Freed) — COMPATIBLE (different depth, same direction).**
These *look* contradictory but reconcile cleanly. Both agree (i) the income tax is inherently an excise and (ii) the 16th "conferred no new power." Champion says the excise is valid and enforceable *as an excise* but narrow in scope; Freed **agrees** the excise is enforceable under Art. I §8 cl.18 and only denies that the *16th Amendment* can support a **direct** tax. Since Champion also denies any valid *direct* unapportioned tax on Americans, the two are aimed at the same target. Freed's "no enforcement clause" is best read as an **additional, more aggressive structural argument layered on top of** Champion's scope argument — a superset, not a contradiction. (Whether it is *correct* is another matter; courts have rejected it — e.g., *In re Becraft* — but it is not internally inconsistent with Champion's thesis.)

**B. §3402 is "a foreign-collection mechanism" (Champion) vs. "a voluntary election" (Freed) — COMPATIBLE (two descriptions of one trap).**
Champion says private-sector withholding is unauthorized because the worker isn't within the covered (foreign-person) class; Freed says it's authorized only *because the worker elected it* via the W-4. These can be squared: both agree the withholding is **not a mandatory imposition on the private worker**, and both make the **worker's signed form** the thing that creates the appearance of liability. Champion's own "there is a completely legal way to sign a W-4 that divests it of legal effect" sits comfortably beside Freed's §3402(n) EXEMPT certificate. Same trap, two vocabularies.

**C. "United States excludes the 50 states" (Freed, strong) vs. Champion's foreign-person emphasis — COMPATIBLE (emphasis, not conflict).**
Champion accepts the territorial definition for FICA and the enclave doctrine; he simply does not make it the fulcrum of the income-tax argument. Freed builds the income tax itself on it. These are **different weightings of a shared premise**, not opposed claims. (One latent tension: Champion's "the tax reaches foreign persons *with U.S.-source income*" presupposes that meaningful U.S.-source activity exists to be taxed, whereas Freed's "tariff only in the territories" pushes the taxable zone offshore/into the enclaves. In edge cases these could point to different taxpayers, but neither book presses the point to a conflict.)

**D. Corporate income tax: "foreign-owned corporations only" (Champion) vs. "domestic corporate form generally" (Freed) — GENUINE CONTRADICTION.**
This is the one place the books cannot both be right. Champion argues (via a delegated-power / "author of the authority" theory and Black's 1933 "doing business" = foreign corporations) that the corporate excise reaches **only foreign-owned** corporations. Freed accepts the orthodox *Flint/Stratton's* reading that the corporate income tax is a valid excise on the **privilege of doing business in corporate form** by **domestic** corporations too — indeed his reform plan proposes taxing domestic **corporate revenues at 3%**, which presupposes a legitimate federal excise on domestic corporations. These are **incompatible theories of the corporate excise's scope**. They do not reconcile: Champion's is the more radical (and idiosyncratic) narrowing; Freed's tracks the mainstream understanding of *Flint*. Notably, this contradiction is *internal to the movement*, not just between the two books.

**E. Judicial integrity vs. judicial authority — COMPATIBLE (complementary).**
"The judges are corrupt" (Champion) and "the courts lack jurisdiction" (Freed) are not mutually exclusive; a proponent could hold both. They are complementary explanations for the same observed fact (defendants lose), aimed at different remedies (nullify the jury vs. dismiss for want of jurisdiction).

**F. The "fringe" theories — CONTRADICTION IN METHOD, and at least one in substance.**
Champion **explicitly rejects** the **861 position** (§861 is "source rules," not the controlling imposition), "expatriation," UCC, zero returns, and "Cracking the Code." Freed does not endorse the 861 label but his **Memorandum of Law** runs a cousin argument through **§3121 "employment"** definitions and the "subject vs. basis" distinction, and he embraces IRS-not-an-agency and Federal-Reserve theories. So:
   - On **method**, the books contradict: one prunes, one accumulates.
   - On **substance**, the clearest clash is that Champion would classify Freed's **IRS-is-not-an-agency** and **payments-to-the-Fed** arguments as the kind of unsupported "mistaken" theory he warns readers away from. (Champion never says so about Freed by name — the books don't cross-reference — but his stated criteria put those claims on the wrong side of his line.)

**Net:** most apparent contradictions dissolve into *differences of depth, emphasis, or vocabulary around a shared thesis*. The **two real ones** are (D) the **scope of the corporate excise** (foreign-only vs. domestic-included) and (F) the **corporate-methodological split** over which movement arguments are legitimate — with Champion's rejected-theory list overlapping several of Freed's embraced arguments.

## One-paragraph verdict

The two books are best understood as **two dialects of a single argument**. They share a thesis (the income tax is a narrow indirect excise that misses the ordinary American), a keystone (§1461 makes only the foreign-person withholding agent liable), a method (definitional literalism + strict construction), and most of their case law. Champion is the **disciplined lumper** — one authorial voice, an "international tax / nontaxpayer" scope theory, and an explicit pruning of the movement's weaker claims. Freed is the **maximalist splitter** — a scripture-framed brief-bank whose distinctive spine is the 16th Amendment's **missing enforcement clause** and the resulting **subject-matter-jurisdiction** attack, garnished with radical add-ons (IRS-not-an-agency, Federal-Reserve, Communist-Manifesto, H.R. 1) that Champion's stated standards would reject. Their apparent contradictions mostly resolve into differences of **depth and emphasis**; the two that do not are the **scope of the corporate excise** (Champion: foreign-owned only; Freed: domestic corporate form generally) and the **methodological split** over which movement theories are legitimate at all. And, to be clear about the world outside these two texts: **every court to consider these theories has rejected them.**
